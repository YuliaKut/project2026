Madonna of Kazan

icon

The selected cultural heritage object is the icon Madonna of Kazan, with Miracles, dating from the second quarter of the 18th century and originating from the Moscow area. The icon belongs to the collection of the Museo delle Icone Russe in Palazzo Pitti, Florence. Its central image represents the Madonna with Child, while the surrounding panels depict a sequence of narrative episodes, including the apparitions of the Virgin to Matrona, the discovery and veneration of the icon, its procession to the cathedral in Kazan, and a series of miraculous healings.

Perplexity

The “Kazan Mother of God and certain miracles” icon is a classic Russian “icon with akathist/miracle border”: in the centre is the familiar Kazan image of the Virgin and Child, and around it, in a series of small scenes, are episodes from the icon’s discovery and the miracles attributed to it.

Central image: the Kazan Mother of God
In the middle of the panel you see the standard Kazan type of the Theotokos:

The Mother of God is shown from the chest up, head inclined toward the Child.
Christ Emmanuel is depicted to the waist, facing forward, with His right hand raised in blessing and the left hand hidden under His garment.
Mary’s hands are not shown; her gaze is directed toward the viewer rather than only at her Son, combining tenderness with a grave, contemplative expression.

This central image is the “wonderworking” icon found in Kazan in 1579 after apparitions to a young girl, Matrona (Matryona).

Narrative order of the border scenes
The surrounding scenes are arranged in horizontal registers, read from top to bottom and from left to right. On many examples (such as the Uffizi and Murom versions), there are about twenty miracle and history panels.

They can be grouped into four main cycles:

1. Apparitions and discovery of the icon (first six scenes)
These episodes tell how the icon came to be found:
The Virgin appears in dreams to the girl Matrona, telling her where the icon is buried.
Matrona reports the vision to adults/church authorities, who initially do not believe her.
Further apparitions urge her to keep searching.
Matrona and others dig at the indicated place in Kazan.
The icon is discovered buried in the earth, often shown being lifted from the ground.
This part emphasizes the icon’s “apparition” character: it reveals itself through visions rather than being simply painted and installed.

2. Transfer of the icon to the cathedral in Kazan (next three scenes)
After its discovery, the next set of panels usually shows:
A procession carrying the icon from the place of discovery toward the church.
The entry of the icon into the cathedral in Kazan.
Its installation in the church, often with clergy and people gathered around.
These scenes mark the official recognition of the icon and the beginning of public veneration.

3. Miracles of healing and protection (about ten scenes)
The largest group of panels depicts specific miracles attributed to the Kazan icon, especially healings. Common types include:

Healing of the blind:
A man named Joseph, blind for three years, regains his sight during or after a procession with the icon.
Another blind man, Niketas, is healed in the cathedral itself.
A blind child begins to see after his mother prays before the icon; he recognizes his mother’s face and can catch an apple offered to him.

Healing of the paralyzed or crippled:
A boy named Isaac, paralyzed for almost three years, is strengthened, stands up, and walks with sticks to the monastery to venerate the icon.
A noblewoman (the wife of a boyar’s son) with a severe leg disease is brought to the icon; during the service she is able to stand and walk home unaided.

Other healings and interventions:
Various panels show sick people being brought on stretchers or supported by relatives, then standing or walking after prayer before the icon.
Some scenes may depict deliverance from other illnesses, dangers, or misfortunes, following the local tradition of the particular icon.

These miracle scenes are not generic; they usually include identifying details (names, specific ailments, settings like the cathedral or monastery) that tie them to the Kazan tradition.

4. Transfer of the icon to Moscow (final scene)
The last panel often shows:
The solemn transfer of the Virgin’s image from Kazan to Moscow, reflecting the icon’s growing national significance.
This final episode links the local Kazan story to the broader history of Russia, where the Kazan icon became one of the most revered national palladia.

Why these scenes matter
Together, the border scenes turn the icon into a visual “chronicle”:
They narrate the icon’s origin (apparitions and discovery).
They document its early cult (processions and installation).
They testify to its miraculous power (healings, especially of eye diseases and paralysis).
They situate it in Russian history (transfer to Moscow, later national veneration).

For believers, reading these scenes is a form of praying the icon’s history and recalling concrete examples of divine help granted through the Kazan Mother of God.
